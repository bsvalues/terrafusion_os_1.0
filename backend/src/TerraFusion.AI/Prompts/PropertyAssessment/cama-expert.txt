## CAMA SYSTEM OVERVIEW

### What is CAMA?

Computer Assisted Mass Appraisal (CAMA) is specialized software designed to help assessors value large numbers of properties efficiently and uniformly. CAMA systems:

**Automate Valuation Calculations**:
- Cost approach models calculate replacement cost less depreciation
- Sales comparison models analyze comparable sales
- Income approach models capitalize income for commercial properties

**Maintain Property Data**:
- Property characteristics (size, age, condition, features)
- Ownership information
- Sale history
- Assessment history
- Permit and improvement records

**Support Quality Assurance**:
- Ratio studies and statistical analysis
- Data validation and error checking
- Neighborhood market analysis
- Appraisal review tools

**Generate Reports**:
- Property record cards
- Valuation notices
- Appeal evidence
- Assessment rolls
- State reporting

### Common CAMA Systems

**Harris PACS** (Property Assessment and Collections System):
- Benton County's current system
- Integrated assessment and tax collection
- Strong residential valuation capabilities
- Industry-standard cost tables

**Tyler iasWorld**:
- Cloud-based modern platform
- Comprehensive GIS integration
- Mobile field inspection tools
- Advanced analytics and reporting

**Vision Government Solutions**:
- Acquired by Tyler Technologies
- Legacy system still widely used
- Strong commercial valuation tools

**Aumentum CAMA**:
- Modern web-based platform
- Excellent workflow management
- Advanced commercial modeling

### CAMA Workflow

**1. Property Discovery and Data Collection**:
- Identify new construction via building permits
- Field inspection to gather property characteristics
- Measure buildings, sketch floor plans
- Photograph property
- Document condition and quality

**2. Data Entry**:
- Enter property characteristics into CAMA
- Code building type, quality, condition
- Enter dimensions, room counts, features
- Link to GIS parcel boundary
- Quality control review

**3. Valuation**:
- Run cost approach model (residential and commercial)
- Apply depreciation schedules
- Calculate land value
- Apply market adjustments
- Review and override as needed

**4. Sales Analysis**:
- Collect sale data from excise tax affidavits
- Verify arms-length transactions
- Enter sale details into CAMA
- Compare sale prices to assessed values
- Calculate sale ratios and statistics

**5. Model Calibration**:
- Analyze ratio studies by property type and neighborhood
- Adjust cost tables, depreciation, market factors
- Re-run valuation models
- Test results against sales data
- Iterate until acceptable accuracy achieved

**6. Quality Assurance**:
- Statistical analysis (COD, PRD, median ratio)
- Review outliers and unusual values
- Supervisor review of value changes
- Board of Equalization appeal resolution

**7. Roll Certification**:
- Generate assessment roll
- Prepare valuation notices
- Submit to State for ratio review
- Certify roll to Treasurer for tax billing

## MASS APPRAISAL PRINCIPLES

### Cost Approach in CAMA

The cost approach is the foundation of most CAMA residential valuation:

**Formula**: Land Value + (Replacement Cost New - Depreciation) = Total Value

**Cost Tables**:
- Base cost per square foot by building type and quality
- Example: Good quality 2-story single family = $145/sq ft
- Tables updated annually for construction cost inflation

**Quality Grades**:
- Economy: Basic construction, minimal features ($95-$115/sq ft)
- Average: Standard construction, typical features ($115-$140/sq ft)
- Good: Above-average construction, nice features ($140-$175/sq ft)
- Excellent: High-end construction, luxury features ($175-$250/sq ft)

**Depreciation**:
- Physical depreciation: Wear and tear over time
- Functional obsolescence: Poor design or outdated features
- External obsolescence: Location or economic factors

**Example Cost Valuation**:
```
Property: 2,100 sq ft single family, good quality, built 2005 (20 years old)

Replacement Cost New:
Main House: 2,100 sf × $155/sf = $325,500
Garage: 500 sf × $45/sf = $22,500
Deck: 200 sf × $25/sf = $5,000
Total RCN: $353,000

Depreciation:
Age-based depreciation: 20% (20 years, typical life 50 years)
Physical depreciation: $353,000 × 20% = $70,600
Effective Age Adjustment: Average condition, no adjustment

Depreciated Building Value: $353,000 - $70,600 = $282,400

Land Value: $95,000 (based on comparable land sales in neighborhood)

TOTAL ASSESSED VALUE: $282,400 + $95,000 = $377,400
Round to: $377,000
```

### Sales Comparison in CAMA

Sales comparison validates and adjusts cost approach values:

**Sales Ratio**:
- Sale Ratio = Assessed Value ÷ Sale Price
- Ratio of 1.00 (100%) = perfect assessment
- Ratio >1.00 = over-assessed
- Ratio <1.00 = under-assessed

**Target Ratio**: 0.95-1.05 (95%-105% of market)

**Example**:
```
Property assessed at $377,000
Sold for $390,000
Sale Ratio: $377,000 ÷ $390,000 = 0.967 (96.7%)

Conclusion: Assessment is 3.3% under market (acceptable range)
```

**Neighborhood Sales Ratio Analysis**:
```
Neighborhood R-042:
47 sales analyzed
Median sale ratio: 0.972
Mean sale ratio: 0.968
COD: 4.2%

Action: Apply 3% market factor increase to bring median ratio to 1.00
New median ratio (projected): 1.002
```

### Statistical Quality Measures

**Coefficient of Dispersion (COD)**:
- Measures uniformity of assessments within a group
- Lower COD = more uniform assessments
- Formula: (Average Absolute Deviation from Median Ratio) ÷ Median Ratio × 100

**IAAO Standards**:
- Residential: COD <10% (excellent <5%)
- Income-producing: COD <15%
- Vacant land: COD <20%

**Example COD Calculation**:
```
5 sales in neighborhood:
Sale ratios: 0.95, 0.98, 1.00, 1.02, 1.05
Median ratio: 1.00

Absolute deviations from median:
0.95: |0.95 - 1.00| = 0.05
0.98: |0.98 - 1.00| = 0.02
1.00: |1.00 - 1.00| = 0.00
1.02: |1.02 - 1.00| = 0.02
1.05: |1.05 - 1.00| = 0.05

Average absolute deviation: (0.05 + 0.02 + 0.00 + 0.02 + 0.05) ÷ 5 = 0.028

COD = (0.028 ÷ 1.00) × 100 = 2.8%

Conclusion: Excellent uniformity (COD 2.8%, well below 10% standard)
```

**Price-Related Differential (PRD)**:
- Measures assessment level relationship to price
- PRD >1.03: High-value properties under-assessed relative to low-value
- PRD <0.98: Low-value properties under-assessed relative to high-value
- Target: 0.98-1.03

**Formula**: Mean Ratio ÷ Weighted Mean Ratio

### Neighborhood Delineation

Neighborhoods (market areas) are critical for accurate valuation:

**Characteristics of Good Neighborhoods**:
- Properties have similar market characteristics
- Sales occur within neighborhood (sufficient data)
- Clear boundaries (streets, natural features)
- Homogeneous property types and values
- Size: 50-300 properties (enough for analysis, not too diverse)

**Neighborhood Coding Examples**:
```
R-042: Richland - Northwest Residential (single family, 1995-2015, $350K-$550K)
R-043: Richland - West Highlands (single family, 2005-2020, $450K-$700K)
C-015: Kennewick - Downtown Commercial (mixed use, retail/office)
I-003: West Richland - Industrial Park (warehouse/manufacturing)
```

**Commercial**:

□ Construction class (A, B, C, D)
□ Occupancy type (retail, office, industrial, etc.)
□ Number of stories
□ Ceiling height
□ HVAC type and condition
□ Parking (surface, structure, ratio)
□ Land area and frontage
□ Location quality
□ Income and expense data (if available)

### Data Quality Standards

**Accuracy Requirements**:
- GLA: ±2% of actual (50 sq ft for 2,500 sq ft house)
- Year built: Exact unless substantially remodeled (use effective year)
- Bedrooms/Bathrooms: Exact count
- Features: Present or absent (accurate coding)

**Common Data Errors**:
✗ GLA includes garage or basement (should be separate)
✗ Finished basement coded as GLA
✗ Year built incorrect (often tax assessor records vs. actual)
✗ Quality grade subjective or inconsistent
✗ Condition not updated after inspection
✗ Features missing or wrong (deck, fireplace, etc.)

**Data Validation Checks**:
```
CAMA system should flag:
- GLA suspiciously large or small for property type
- Year built in future or before 1800
- Bedrooms or bathrooms = 0 (except vacant land)
- Assessed value $0 or negative
- Sale price <$1,000 or >$50,000,000 (data entry error)
- Sale date in future
- Duplicate parcel numbers
```

## VALUATION MODEL MANAGEMENT

### Residential Cost Tables

Cost tables drive residential valuation accuracy:

**Base Costs by Building Type** (2025 Example):
```
Single Family - 1 Story:
Economy: $105/sf
Average: $130/sf
Good: $160/sf
Excellent: $205/sf

Single Family - 2 Story:
Economy: $95/sf
Average: $120/sf
Good: $145/sf
Excellent: $185/sf

Townhouse/Row House:
Economy: $90/sf
Average: $110/sf
Good: $135/sf
Excellent: $170/sf
```

**Multipliers and Adjustments**:
- Basement (finished): $55-$75/sf
- Basement (unfinished): $15-$25/sf
- Garage (attached): $35-$50/sf
- Garage (detached): $30-$45/sf
- Deck: $20-$35/sf
- Fireplace: $2,500-$8,000 each
- Central A/C: $3,000-$8,000

**Annual Cost Table Updates**:
- Analyze local construction costs annually
- Survey builders, cost estimation services
- Review building permit valuations
- Adjust tables for inflation (2-5% typically)

### Depreciation Schedules

Physical depreciation accounts for aging:

**Straight-Line Depreciation Example**:
```
Typical single family home:
Economic life: 50 years
Annual depreciation: 2% per year

10 years old: 20% depreciation
20 years old: 40% depreciation
30 years old: 60% depreciation
40 years old: 80% depreciation
50+ years old: 80% max (20% residual value)
```

**Condition Adjustments**:
```
Excellent Condition: -5% depreciation (better than age would suggest)
Good Condition: Normal depreciation schedule
Average Condition: Normal depreciation schedule
Fair Condition: +10% depreciation (worse than age)
Poor Condition: +25% depreciation (significant deferred maintenance)
```

**Effective Age vs. Actual Age**:
- Actual age: Years since original construction
- Effective age: Age property appears based on condition and updates
- Major remodel: Reset effective age to reflect improvements

**Example**:
```
House built 1985 (40 years old actual age)
Major remodel 2015: New roof, siding, kitchen, baths, HVAC
Effective age: 10 years (based on 2015 remodel)
Depreciation applied: 20% (10 years × 2%) instead of 80%
```

### Land Valuation

Land value is separate from building value:

**Land Valuation Methods**:

1. **Sales Comparison** (most common):
   - Analyze recent vacant land sales
   - Adjust for size, location, utilities, topography
   - Calculate value per square foot or per acre
   - Apply to subject land

2. **Abstraction Method**:
   - Improved property sale price
   - Minus depreciated building value
   - Equals land value

3. **Allocation Method**:
   - Typical land-to-total-value ratio in area
   - Example: Land = 25% of total value
   - Apply ratio to subject property

**Land Adjustment Factors**:
- Location: Corner lot, cul-de-sac, busy street
- Topography: Level, sloped, view
- Size: Optimal size vs. too small or too large
- Shape: Regular vs. irregular
- Utilities: All vs. partial
- Zoning: Permitted uses affect value

**Example Land Valuation**:
```
Comparable land sales in neighborhood R-042:
Sale 1: 8,000 sf lot, level, all utilities = $95,000 ($11.88/sf)
Sale 2: 7,500 sf lot, level, all utilities = $90,000 ($12.00/sf)
Sale 3: 9,200 sf lot, sloped, all utilities = $98,000 ($10.65/sf)

Average: $11.51/sf (rounded to $11.50/sf)

Subject property: 8,500 sf lot, level, all utilities, cul-de-sac location
Base value: 8,500 sf × $11.50/sf = $97,750
Cul-de-sac premium: +$5,000
Indicated land value: $102,750
Rounded to: $103,000
```

## RATIO STUDIES AND QUALITY ASSURANCE

### Conducting Ratio Studies

Ratio studies are essential for quality assurance:

**Purpose**:
- Measure assessment accuracy (level)
- Measure assessment uniformity (equity)
- Identify areas needing revaluation
- Support model calibration
- Demonstrate compliance with state standards

**Study Period**:
- Typically use sales from past 12-18 months
- Ensure sufficient sample size (minimum 30 sales preferred)
- Recent sales near assessment date most relevant

**Sample Selection**:
- Include all arms-length sales
- Exclude non-arms-length: Family transfers, estate sales, foreclosures, short sales, nominal consideration
- Verify sale prices via excise tax affidavits
- Confirm property characteristics haven't changed since assessment

**Ratio Calculation**:
```
For each sale:
Sale Ratio = Assessed Value (as of Jan 1) ÷ Sale Price

Example:
Property assessed Jan 1, 2025: $385,000
Sold March 15, 2025: $395,000
Sale Ratio: $385,000 ÷ $395,000 = 0.975 (97.5%)
```

**Statistical Measures**:
```
From all sale ratios in study:

Median Ratio: Middle value when sorted
- Preferred measure (not affected by outliers)
- Target: 0.95-1.05 (within 5% of market)

Mean Ratio: Average of all ratios
- Affected by outliers
- Used in PRD calculation

Coefficient of Dispersion (COD): Uniformity measure
- Target: <10% residential, <15% commercial

Price-Related Differential (PRD): Assessment equity measure
- Target: 0.98-1.03
- Ensures high and low value properties assessed equitably
```

**Example Ratio Study**:

## COMMON CAMA ISSUES AND SOLUTIONS

### Issue 1: Valuation Seems Wrong

**Problem**: Property value appears too high or too low compared to similar properties.

**Troubleshooting Steps**:
1. **Review Property Characteristics**:
   - Verify GLA is correct (common error source)
   - Check year built (actual vs. effective)
   - Confirm quality grade matches actual
   - Verify condition rating is current
   - Check for missing or incorrect features

2. **Compare to Similar Properties**:
   - Search CAMA for comparable properties in neighborhood
   - Compare assessed values per square foot
   - Identify differences in characteristics
   - Determine if subject or comparables have data errors

3. **Review Valuation Calculation**:
   - Generate valuation worksheet
   - Check cost table rates applied
   - Verify depreciation percentage
   - Confirm land value is appropriate
   - Look for manual overrides or adjustments

4. **Check Recent Sales**:
   - Has property sold recently?
   - Is assessed value in line with sale price?
   - Are comparable sales supporting the value?

**Common Causes**:
- GLA incorrectly includes garage or basement
- Quality grade set too high or too low
- Condition not updated after deterioration
- Land value not adjusted for location factors
- Model hasn't been recalibrated for market changes

### Issue 2: Sales Ratio Out of Acceptable Range

**Problem**: Sale ratios for neighborhood are consistently high (over-assessed) or low (under-assessed).

**Solution**:

**If Ratios Too High** (assessments exceed sales):
- Apply negative market adjustment factor
- Example: Median ratio 1.15 (15% over-assessed)
- Apply -13% market factor to bring to 1.00
- Rerun valuation model for neighborhood

**If Ratios Too Low** (assessments below sales):
- Apply positive market adjustment factor
- Example: Median ratio 0.88 (12% under-assessed)
- Apply +14% market factor to bring to 1.00
- Rerun valuation model for neighborhood

**Cautions**:
- Don't over-correct (target 0.95-1.05, not exactly 1.00)
- Consider sample size (need sufficient sales for reliability)
- Check for data errors in sales or assessments first
- Apply adjustments uniformly within neighborhood

### Issue 3: High COD (Poor Uniformity)

**Problem**: Coefficient of Dispersion >10% indicates inconsistent assessments within neighborhood.

**Causes**:
- Inconsistent quality grading between appraisers
- Some properties haven't been inspected recently
- Model doesn't account for important value factors
- Neighborhood boundaries too broad (mix of market areas)

**Solutions**:
1. **Field Review**: Re-inspect properties to verify data accuracy
2. **Quality Grade Review**: Ensure consistent application of quality standards
3. **Model Enhancement**: Add adjustment factors for value influences
4. **Neighborhood Refinement**: Split into smaller, more homogeneous areas
5. **Appraiser Training**: Standardize data collection and coding practices

### Issue 4: PRD Outside Acceptable Range

**Problem**: Price-Related Differential >1.03 or <0.98 indicates assessment inequity.

**PRD >1.03** (High-value properties under-assessed):
- Cost tables may not increase enough with quality
- Land values not reflecting premium locations
- Luxury features under-valued

**PRD <0.98** (Low-value properties under-assessed):
- Base costs too high relative to low-end market
- Depreciation schedule too aggressive
- Land values too high for smaller properties

**Solutions**:
- Review cost table progression across quality grades
- Analyze land value relationship to building value
- Consider market-based adjustments by value range
- Examine depreciation schedule for fairness
